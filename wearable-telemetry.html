Distributed Wearable Autonomic Telemetry as a Macro-Social Sensor Array: Evolutionary Calibration, Spatio-Temporal Phase Synchronisation, and Institutional Observability

Henrik Hagnell

Standalone research and architecture paper · 18 November 2026 · ORCID 0000-0002-5324-5779

Abstract

Conventional institutional indicators of social stability, organisational cohesion, and environmental friction—such as macroeconomic aggregates, crime reports, waiting-list metrics, and opinion surveys—suffer from severe reporting lag, spatial aggregation, denominator manipulation, and declarative norm compliance. In contrast, the mammalian neuro-autonomic system is an evolutionarily conserved, continuously active sensor that samples micro-environmental predictability, physical bottlenecks, and conspecific stress cues without conscious mediation. With the global proliferation of inexpensive, continuous 24-hour commercial wearable devices (smartwatches, fitness bands, and smart rings) connected to smartphone geolocation and cloud infrastructures, it is now physically possible to aggregate multi-modal physiological telemetry—specifically photoplethysmography-derived Heart Rate Variability (PPG-HRV), accelerometry, skin temperature, and sleep architecture—across millions of individuals. This paper investigates the scientific mechanisms, signal-processing pipelines, predictive capabilities, and hard physical and institutional limits of combining calibrated human biological sensors into a real-time, macro-level telescope for monitoring community strain and forecasting social trajectories. We demonstrate how within-subject longitudinal calibration separates individual reactivity phenotypes along the Differential Susceptibility and Adaptive Calibration continua—distinguishing high-gain early-warning detectors ("orchids"), buffered baselines ("dandelions"), and chronically blunted profiles (ACM Type IV). We then trace how spatial and topological aggregation cancels idiosyncratic physiological noise while amplifying shared environmental forcing and interpersonal contagion via common-noise phase contraction (the Teramae–Tanaka mechanism), Kuramoto order parameters, and self-supervised foundation models. Finally, drawing on comparative empirical architectures from both the Western and Chinese societal systems, we evaluate the hard constraints of non-random motion dropout, mathematical non-invertibility, network autocorrelation, and Goodhart spoofing, detailing how incumbent and challenger institutional actors can operationalise distributed biological telemetry.

Keywords: wearable sensing; heart-rate variability; photoplethysmography; population telemetry; spatio-temporal synchronisation; institutional observability; signal processing.
