Social Trust as an Observational Sensor for Institutional Niche Dynamics

Research papers and citations

Henrik Hagnell · Revised manuscript · 15 November 2026 · Companion collection DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24248701 (shared across seven papers and the overview)

Abstract

Social trust is frequently represented either as a generalised attitude or as an immaterial stock that reduces the cost of institutional coordination. Neither representation is adequate for identifying divergence between the declared continuity of a long-lived institution and the physical reliability of the human niche on which it depends. This paper formalises trust as a domain-specific, directional observational sensor operating on three response times: autonomic detection (τautonomic), pragmatic reliance and verification (τsig), and inherited declarative schemas (τschema). It preserves the Dissipative Structure Theory–Niche Construction Theory (DST–NCT) state-space x(t) = (b(t), m(t), a(t), u(t)), without treating trust as a fifth fundamental state or an exergy stock. A history-dependent observation operator links the three clocks to physical exposure, care provisioning, network relations and institutional information. The model derives conditional somatic suppression costs, a recovery-dependent allostatic burden, heterogeneous public-recognition thresholds and hysteretic coordination transitions. It explicitly separates these informational transitions from physical separatrix crossings, relational trust from coercive assurance, and confidence from actual buffer coverage. A hardened five-domain telemetry matrix addresses administrative gaming, denominator changes and physiological confounding. A comparative test at t0 = 2026 uses Sweden, the United Kingdom and the United States to distinguish schema-locked divergence, externally coupled reliance and heterogeneous modular buffering. The country portraits are conditional structural interpretations anchored to identified observations, not measured national three-clock scores. Falsification conditions and an empirical research design specify what evidence would establish discriminatory power beyond existing trust surveys and macroeconomic accounting.
