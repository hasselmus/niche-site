Terms and concepts

Short explanations of recurring terms in the framework. Entries consolidate the chapters’ term sections and definitions used elsewhere in their text. They describe proposed mechanisms; a named mechanism is not, by itself, evidence that its conditions hold in a particular case.

Based on the framework draft dated 21 October 2026. Chapter references identify the source sections. These are shortened explanations, not quotations. For notation, see Variables.

Physical and niche dynamics

Dissipative structure
An organised pattern maintained away from equilibrium by continued throughput and irreversible processes. Ch. 1
Thermodynamic driving force
A physical disequilibrium, such as a temperature or chemical-potential difference, that drives irreversible processes. Ch. 1
Exergy
Maximum obtainable work relative to a specified reference environment; not automatically accessible to organisms or institutions. Ch. 1
Stock and flux
A stock is an accumulated quantity; a flux is its transfer rate. Their units must not be conflated. Ch. 1
Niche construction
Organism-mediated alteration of environmental conditions that can change later activity and selection. Ch. 1
Ecological inheritance
Effects of modified environments passed to subsequent generations, distinct from genetic inheritance. Ch. 1
Evolutionary niche
The selection pressures experienced by a population, not an institutional state-space basin. Ch. 1
Steady state
Constant state variables with potentially continuing flows; distinct from thermodynamic equilibrium. Ch. 1
Stored exergy
A reserve of usable exergy that can be discharged later, represented by B(t). Ch. 3
Structural state
A persistent material arrangement or service capacity represented by s(t), not the energy originally expended to build it. Ch. 3
Buffering
Use of a reserve to cover a temporary throughput shortfall. Ch. 3
Pulse mobilisation
Short-duration activity exceeding ordinary contemporaneous throughput, supplied by previously accumulated capacity. Ch. 3
Structural conversion
Expenditure of exergy, materials and labour to change persistent structures and future flow conditions. Ch. 3
Maintenance
Continuing or periodic throughput that preserves or restores structural function. Ch. 3
Deterioration
Loss of material function or service capacity through wear, exposure, use or other physical mechanisms. Ch. 3
Replenishment
Restoration of a depleted reserve or structural capacity through new exergy inflow, repair or replacement. Ch. 3
Delivery-power constraint
A store can be sufficient in quantity but unable to deliver the required exergy at the necessary rate. Ch. 3
Temporal buffering
Use of reserves and durable structures to accommodate shortfalls, variability and mismatched process durations. Ch. 3

Institutional and economic mechanisms

Institutional extrapolation
Encoding historically contingent material conditions as though they were permanently available. Ch. 2
Niche lock
Mutual specialisation of institutional rules and material structures that raises the cost or duration of changing regime. Ch. 2, 4
Temporal mismatch
Fast operational or claim-adjustment demands outrunning physical regeneration and institutional adaptation. Ch. 2
Representational divergence
Growing mismatch between institutional measurements or expectations and the underlying physical state. Ch. 2
Financialisation
Increasing reliance on financial claims, valuations and allocation mechanisms to organise present and future activity. Ch. 2
Claim–substrate conflation
Mistaking monetary value, liquidity or claims for the physical availability, substitutability or timely delivery of resources. Ch. 2, 5
Structural cannibalisation
Sustaining current institutional commitments through reserve depletion or under-maintenance, consumption or repurposing of the structures needed for continued operation. Ch. 2
Rate-induced tipping
A regime transition occurring when forcing or material change exceeds feasible institutional adjustment speed. Ch. 2
Verification overhead
Labour, information and throughput needed to audit, monitor and enforce coordination as trust becomes less reliable. Ch. 2
Financial claim
An institutionally recognised relation governing present or future command over resources; not a physical stock. Ch. 3, 5
Claims space
The part of institutional state a(t) that records entitlements, liabilities and claims on routing capacity. Ch. 5
Economic representation
Prices, accounts, valuations and analytic categories describing selected relations within a coupled system. Ch. 5
Cross-domain coupling
A causal link between domains such as exergy, labour, structures and claims without making their units interchangeable. Ch. 5
Representational compression
Using a small set of correlated indicators to coordinate processes that remain physically heterogeneous. Ch. 5
Claim realisation
Conversion of an recognised claim into actual redirected labour, exergy, materials or access. Ch. 5
Claim resolution
Revaluation, rescheduling, dilution, default or other adjustment of claims that cannot jointly be realised. Ch. 5
Symbolic temporal reach
The ability to issue and coordinate long-horizon claims before the future physical flows they anticipate exist. Ch. 5
Partial economic observation
A metric that represents selected institutional activity while omitting other material, biological or relational states. Ch. 6
Metric–substrate divergence
A difference in direction between an economic indicator and the material or biological condition relevant to interpreting it. Ch. 6
Boundary displacement
Preserving a measured local position by shifting physical demands, exposures or dependence beyond its accounting boundary. Ch. 6
Cross-metric divergence
Different indicators moving apart because they measure distinct domains or timescales. Ch. 6
Externality
An effect propagating beyond the transactions, prices or accounting boundaries that represent the initiating activity. Ch. 7
Capacity-enhancing productivity
Measured output/input gains associated with higher feasible future service or reduced required throughput. Ch. 7
Capacity-consuming productivity
Measured output/input gains partly achieved through reserve depletion, deferred maintenance or transferred physical costs. Ch. 7
Relational trust
Conditional expectation of reliable reciprocal conduct and claims, reducing verification and enforcement overhead when materially supported. Ch. 2
Trust scale-transition
Re-routing cooperation from low-verification macro-institutional channels to smaller networks with denser reciprocal verification. Ch. 2

Basins, representation and scientific knowledge

Incumbent basin (N₀)
Region of coupled state-space in which a particular institutional configuration can continue reproducing. Ch. 4
Separatrix (Σ)
Effective boundary between dynamical regimes; crossings can exhibit hysteresis or difficult reversal. Ch. 4
Successor regime (Nᵢ)
A post-transition configuration potentially supporting continued human organisation under different feedbacks and constraints. Ch. 4
Regime-neutral viability
Assessing human and socio-ecological viability separately from the viability of an incumbent institution. Ch. 4
Successor-state exclusion
Rich modelling of an incumbent regime and its failure while viable post-incumbent configurations remain sparsely represented. Ch. 4
Boundary vigilance
Concentrated observation and intervention focused on approaching the incumbent basin boundary. Ch. 4
Institutional coordinate system
The rules, categories, measurements and relations through which an institution represents and organises its environment. Ch. 4
Collective basin-loss threat
Proposed concern about the future continuation of a recognised group or institutional configuration. Ch. 4
Basin-loss salience
Prominence of a potential loss of incumbent configuration in collective perception and coordination. Ch. 4
Collective angst
Established social-psychological construct concerning an ingroup's expected future vitality. Ch. 4
Perceived collective continuity
Perception that a group persists through historical and cultural time. Ch. 4
Temporal continuity
Expectation that future collective activity remains sufficiently legible for present commitments to connect with it. Ch. 4
Successor-state legibility
Extent to which post-boundary arrangements have identifiable actors, resource flows, constraints and procedures. Ch. 4
Endogenous epistemic trap
Feedback in which weak successor representation increases basin-loss threat and incumbent-centred research, further restricting successor coverage. Ch. 4, 8
Threat rigidity
Restriction of information processing and control patterns under perceived threat. Ch. 4
Collective resilience
Coordination and mutual support that can emerge under common danger. Ch. 4
Continuity technology
Narratives, rituals, rehearsed procedures or succession arrangements that make future collective states legible. Ch. 4
Transformability
Capacity to create or enter a fundamentally different functional regime; distinct from preserving an existing one. Ch. 4
Normal science
Specialised problem-solving under a shared disciplinary matrix. Ch. 8
Scientific niche
Material, biological and institutional conditions under which a scientific community and its methods reproduce. Ch. 8
Epistemic coverage
Range of states, questions and transitions represented and investigated by a knowledge system. Ch. 8
Institutional research selection
Distribution of resources, recognition and infrastructure among research questions. Ch. 8
Boundary-coupled epistemic selection
Changing research coverage as the prospect of losing an incumbent basin becomes salient. Ch. 8

Biological adaptation, succession and care

Life-history allocation
Allocation of limited metabolic energy and time among development, maintenance, mating, reproduction and care. Ch. 9
Biological representational compression
Projection of multidimensional life-history trade-offs onto a simplified fast–slow continuum. Ch. 9
Embodied capital
Accumulated somatic, cognitive and skill capacities developed over extended periods of provisioning. Ch. 9
Developmental niche construction
Material and institutional arrangements shaping the developmental environment and resulting phenotypic calibration. Ch. 9
Environmental sensitivity (DSM)
Continuously varying responsiveness to environmental conditions; the framework invokes differential susceptibility. Ch. 9
Adaptive calibration (ACM)
Calibration of stress-response and behavioural profiles to developmental unpredictability and security. Ch. 9
Slow life-history allocation
Long-horizon investment in development, maintenance and embodied capital under reliable support. Ch. 9
Horizon truncation
Shortening of planning or investment horizons as delayed commitments become less reliable. Ch. 9, 12
Demographic decoupling
Withdrawal from the long-duration labour, debt and reproductive pathways required to reproduce the incumbent regime; distinct from population disappearance. Ch. 9
Common-mode environmental compression
A shared environmental constraint that aligns previously dispersed behavioural and demographic adjustments. Ch. 9
High-plasticity vanguard
The more environmentally responsive tail of a population that registers common constraints early, without implying a homogeneous response. Ch. 9
Perceived reproductive continuity
Social observation of younger cohorts progressing through maturation, household formation and reproduction. Ch. 10
Recognised succession field
Younger individuals whose expected continuation provides continuity cues to a given observer or network. Ch. 10
Reproductive support network
Kin and non-kin provisioning, care and material access supporting juvenile development and household reproduction. Ch. 10
Shared observation (common knowledge)
Awareness within a trust network that others are observing the same succession cues. Ch. 10
Demographic hysteresis
Path-dependent difference between the threshold generating collective alarm and the stronger sustained recovery required to reverse it. Ch. 10
Parakin placebo / synthetic continuity
Substitution of mediated or curated succession signals for locally sustained biological continuity and support. Ch. 10
Health-risk buffering
Provisioning of people through illness, injury, disability and old age during periods of low or negative net metabolic production. Ch. 11
Care boundary (Σcare)
Proposed threshold at which collective recognition of persistently unfulfilled care commitments changes institutional cooperation. Ch. 11
Kin-buffer atrophy
Long-term erosion of informal caregiving capacities through institutional substitution, spatial separation and demographic change. Ch. 11
Camouflaged care triage
Rationing care through queues, administrative opacity and altered procedures while maintaining formal claims. Ch. 11
Autonomous exit reframing
Institutional representation of structurally constrained care or housing situations as voluntary private choices. Ch. 11
Intergenerational care pincer
Reinforcing tension in which extraction from younger cohorts and reduced credible eldercare buffering erode both demographic incorporation and confidence. Ch. 11

Decoupling and successor formation

Centrifugal decoupling
Outward withdrawal of cohorts from an incumbent's labour, claims and reproductive pipelines into smaller or informal networks. Ch. 12
Centripetal decoupling
In-situ horizon compression among actors within institutional networks, shifting effort towards immediate extraction. Ch. 12
Fiduciary defection
Re-routing claims or buffers from impersonal institutional maintenance to bounded kin, patronage or coalition networks. Ch. 12
Unbuffered patch foraging
Short-horizon exploitation of distribution channels whose verification capacity has deteriorated. Ch. 12
Low-exergy coercive containment
Substitution of relatively low-overhead enforcement for more resource-intensive buffering and coordination. Ch. 12
Chimeric institution
Configuration retaining the legal/extractive exterior of an older regime while internal flows are reorganised around short-horizon extraction. Ch. 12
Modular autarkic fragmentation
Formation of smaller, lower-overhead successor niches by detaching material and administrative modules from the incumbent centre. Ch. 12
Reverse demographic hysteresis
Renewed slow-horizon coordination in a buffered successor niche after sustained generational progress crosses the recovery threshold. Ch. 12
Successor nucleation
Local establishment of a new functional configuration and its own resource, coordination and demographic feedbacks. Ch. 12

Conflict and scenario analysis

Coupled antagonistic perturbation
Physical conflict in which configurations expend resources to degrade one another's material and biological bases. Ch. 13
Uncoupled exogenous perturbation
Acute environmental shock entering from external conditions without an opposing institutional actor. Ch. 13
Kinetic destruction
Direct loss of stocks, infrastructure service or biological capacity from physical force. Ch. 13
Logistics interdiction
Disruption of routing or delivery rates despite the existence of physical stocks elsewhere. Ch. 13
Schelling coercion (latent violence)
Credible signalling of future destruction that induces anticipatory resource diversion. Ch. 13
Clausewitzian friction
Mismatch between compressed operational models and interacting physical and biological conditions. Ch. 13
Total basin destruction
Damage eliminating biological substrates or feasible successor states, rather than merely replacing the incumbent regime. Ch. 13
Analytical present
Declared time t₀ at which an inherited, path-dependent state is reconstructed for scenario work. Ch. 14
Inherited-state reconstruction
Estimation of causally relevant current stocks, structures, population conditions and institutional obligations. Ch. 14
Observational multiplicity
Coexistence of valid but differently bounded, dated or positioned observations of the same coupled system. Ch. 14
Temporal-frame non-equivalence
Distinction among event, observation, information, publication, commitment, planning and physical-availability times. Ch. 14
Compatible-state set
Set of candidate present-state reconstructions consistent with admitted evidence, assumptions and physical constraints. Ch. 14
Selective compression
Aggregation permitted only when it preserves the causal differences material to the scenario. Ch. 14
State uncertainty
Unknown condition of variables already represented in the application. Ch. 14
Model uncertainty
Unknown parameters or mechanisms within the chosen model representation. Ch. 14
Epistemic non-closure
No warrant that the represented scenario set includes all physically accessible configurations. Ch. 14
Directional neutrality
No inference about direction or probability of missing outcomes from unidentified omissions alone. Ch. 14
Coverage criticism
Examination of whether the selected observers, boundaries, categories or regimes omit relevant states. Ch. 14
Retrospective assimilation
Treating an unexpected development as if it had already been predicted by an imprecise earlier scenario. Ch. 14
